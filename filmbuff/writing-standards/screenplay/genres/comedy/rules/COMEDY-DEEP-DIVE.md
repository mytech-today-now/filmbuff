## Overview

Deep-reference guide for professional comedy screenwriting and AI-assisted
generation. Covers the theories, structures, joke mechanics, and tonal
sub-genres required to produce consistently funny, emotionally grounded
material. See also: [comedy.md](./comedy.md) for the base rule set this
document extends.

## Core Concept

Comedy is the art of controlled violation that delivers pleasure through
surprise, recognition, and safety. A script earns laughs when stakes feel
real to the character, feel ultimately safe to the audience, and every
joke advances story or character rather than standing alone.

## Comedy Fundamentals & Philosophy

Apply the four theories in combination; the strongest jokes hit several
at once. Comedy honors an emotional contract: stakes must feel real to
the character but never truly catastrophic to the protagonist (unless
the sub-genre is dark), and every laugh should advance character or plot.

### Incongruity

- **Description**: Establish a clear pattern, then shatter it logically
  but unexpectedly.
- **Why It Matters**: Expectation vs. reality is the oldest reliable
  laugh engine; without a pattern, there is no break to enjoy.
- **Examples**:
  - ✅ **Good**: Slacker worldview dragged into kidnapping-plot stakes.
  - ❌ **Bad**: Random weirdness with no prior pattern to violate.
- **Film Reference**: The Big Lebowski (1998) -- a rug replacement
  escalates into nihilists, ransom, and a bowling league.

### Superiority

- **Description**: Let the audience feel smarter than a character
  without losing empathy for them.
- **Why It Matters**: We laugh at benign flaws; unrelatable targets
  read as cruel.
- **Examples**:
  - ✅ **Good**: Rigid logic creates self-inflicted social disasters we
    also recognize in ourselves.
  - ❌ **Bad**: Punching at a target the audience cannot identify with.
- **Film Reference**: Curb Your Enthusiasm -- Larry David's principled
  stubbornness detonates every room he enters.

### Relief

- **Description**: Stack escalating awkwardness or danger, then release
  with a surprising but safe payoff.
- **Why It Matters**: Built-up tension makes the exhale louder;
  laughter is the discharge.
- **Examples**:
  - ✅ **Good**: A dinner-table lie accumulates until an absurd
    revelation pops the pressure.
  - ❌ **Bad**: Releasing tension before it is built, or never at all.
- **Film Reference**: Meet the Parents (2000) -- the doctor-lie dinner.

### Benign Violation

- **Description**: Threaten something the audience cares about, then
  reframe it as safe; high personal stakes plus objective safety is
  the fertile ground.
- **Why It Matters**: Safety + violation = laugh. Remove safety and it
  becomes horror; remove violation and it is boring.
- **Examples**:
  - ✅ **Good**: A mortifying public humiliation that harms no one
    permanently.
  - ❌ **Bad**: Real, lasting harm played for laughs without reframing.
- **Film Reference**: Bridesmaids (2011) -- humiliation without cruelty.

## Comedy Structure & Architecture

**Macro (Three-Act Comedic Arc)**:

- **Act 1 (Setup)**: Normal world, protagonist's comedic wound, and the
  comedic premise -- the engine incongruity. *Groundhog Day* -- cynical
  weatherman + time loop.
- **Act 2 (Escalation)**: Premise intensifies; comedy ladder where each
  rung tops the last. The midpoint is the "all is lost" comedic low --
  often the funniest beat because it is the most desperate.
- **Act 3 (Resolution)**: Protagonist inverts the original flaw; a final
  button ties back to the premise. *Bridesmaids* -- the spiral peaks at
  the plane disaster, then resolves through earned friendship.

### Escalation Curve

- **Description**: Each comedic sequence should feel 10-30% bigger than
  the previous one; track a "laugh floor" that never drops below the
  previous peak.
- **Why It Matters**: A flat laugh floor reads as a deflating second act.
- **Examples**:
  - ✅ **Good**: Small lie -> bigger lie -> elaborate scheme -> chaos.
  - ❌ **Bad**: Biggest set piece at page 30 with nowhere to go.
- **Film Reference**: Liar Liar (1997) -- lies escalate from minor to
  catastrophic.

### Setup / Punchline

- **Description**: The classic two-beat spine: premise -> setup ->
  punchline -> optional tag.
- **Why It Matters**: Every other structure is a variant of this shape.
- **Examples**:
  - ✅ **Good**: Tight, specific setup; cut any word after the landing.
  - ❌ **Bad**: Setup that already implies the punchline.
- **Film Reference**: Airplane! (1980) -- the whole script at scale.

### Rule of Three

- **Description**: Two similar beats establish a pattern; the third
  breaks or tops it.
- **Why It Matters**: Two is predictable, four is exhausting; three is
  the rhythmic sweet spot.
- **Examples**:
  - ✅ **Good**: "That's what she said" delivered twice straight, then
    in an absurd context.
  - ❌ **Bad**: Same joke repeated five times with no variation.
- **Film Reference**: Hot Fuzz (2007) -- "the greater good" in triplicate.

### Callback

- **Description**: An earlier innocuous line or prop returns transformed
  by new context.
- **Why It Matters**: Rewards attention; compresses time by carrying
  prior meaning into a new beat.
- **Examples**:
  - ✅ **Good**: A throwaway in Act 1 becomes the Act 3 button.
  - ❌ **Bad**: Repeating a line without new meaning or stakes.
- **Film Reference**: Arrested Development -- "there's always money in
  the banana stand."

### Escalation Ladder

- **Description**: A single joke builds across multiple lines or scenes,
  each rung higher than the last.
- **Why It Matters**: Sustains laugh momentum and builds theme.
- **Examples**:
  - ✅ **Good**: Social gaffe -> public shame -> legal risk ->
    existential crisis, all driven by one character flaw.
  - ❌ **Bad**: Rungs that swap topics mid-climb.
- **Film Reference**: The Office (US) -- the Scott's Tots reveal.

### Button Ending

- **Description**: A final punch after a scene has apparently ended.
- **Why It Matters**: Sends the audience out on the strongest beat and
  closes tonal loops.
- **Examples**:
  - ✅ **Good**: Dialogue settles, character turns, last line lands.
  - ❌ **Bad**: Two or three buttons in a row that dilute each other.
- **Film Reference**: Curb Your Enthusiasm -- nearly every episode-out.

## How to Write a Joke

**Joke anatomy (page level)**: Premise (assumption) -> Setup
(information) -> Punchline (violation) -> Tag (optional topper). Plant
every major punchline at least 3 pages before its payoff (ideally 8-15).

### Misdirection

- **Description**: Lead the audience toward one reading, then reveal
  another.
- **Why It Matters**: Surprise without logic is randomness; misdirection
  preserves retroactive sense.
- **Examples**:
  - ✅ **Good**: A line plays literal and metaphorical at once.
  - ❌ **Bad**: A reveal the setup never pointed at.
- **Film Reference**: Arrested Development -- "there's always money in
  the banana stand."

### Double Meanings & Wordplay

- **Description**: Exploit ambiguity so two meanings fire simultaneously.
- **Why It Matters**: Density without length; two jokes in one breath.
- **Examples**:
  - ✅ **Good**: A single line lands for both the character and us.
  - ❌ **Bad**: Puns that stop the scene to explain themselves.
- **Film Reference**: 30 Rock -- rapid-fire homonym play.

### Reversals

- **Description**: Set an expectation, deliver the opposite.
- **Why It Matters**: Cleanest form of incongruity; cheap to write, hard
  to earn.
- **Examples**:
  - ✅ **Good**: Tough cop reveals a gentle hobby mid-interrogation.
  - ❌ **Bad**: Reversal that contradicts established character.
- **Film Reference**: 21 Jump Street (2012) -- genre expectations flipped.

### Anti-Jokes

- **Description**: Deliver literal truth where absurdity is expected.
- **Why It Matters**: The refusal to joke becomes the joke; works only
  when the pattern is clearly set.
- **Examples**:
  - ✅ **Good**: Character answers a surreal prompt with earnest data.
  - ❌ **Bad**: Flat literalism without a pattern to defy.
- **Film Reference**: The Simpsons -- Troy McClure's painful literal
  reading of "I'm not a doctor, but I play one on TV."

### Layering

- **Description**: Stack multiple violations (incongruity + superiority
  + relief) into a single line.
- **Why It Matters**: The best jokes in the canon hit several theories
  at once.
- **Examples**:
  - ✅ **Good**: A line that surprises, flatters our insight, and
    releases tension together.
  - ❌ **Bad**: Single-note jokes that leave craft on the table.
- **Film Reference**: Succession -- insults that sting, characterize,
  and advance plot.

**Generating premises**: Start with a rigid worldview; ask what situation
forces it into maximum conflict with reality; plant evidence early so the
payoff feels earned.

## How to Land a Joke

### Rhythm on the Page

- **Description**: Short punchlines land harder; use white space and
  parentheticals for beats.
- **Why It Matters**: Reaction shots and silences often outscore the
  joke itself.
- **Examples**:
  - ✅ **Good**: Three-word punch followed by a silent reaction beat.
  - ❌ **Bad**: Punchline followed by explanatory dialogue.
- **Film Reference**: Anchorman (2004) -- the newscast desk-flip reaction.

### Visual vs. Verbal

- **Description**: Precise language for verbal jokes; exact framing and
  action for visual gags.
- **Why It Matters**: Vague action lines flatten physical comedy; vague
  dialogue dulls wit.
- **Examples**:
  - ✅ **Good**: Action line names the prop, the angle, and the beat.
  - ❌ **Bad**: "They do something funny with the cake."
- **Film Reference**: The Naked Gun franchise -- specificity is the gag.

**Performance notes format**:

```fountain
RON BURGUNDY
(stunned, then recovering with false bravado)
Well... that escalated quickly.
```

**Editing for laughs**:

- Cut any line after the punch that explains it.
- Never run two punchlines back-to-back without a reaction beat.
- Strongest laughs often come from a character's refusal to acknowledge
  the joke.
- Validation: insert `[BEAT]` markers and rate each joke's setup-to-punch
  rhythm on a 1-10 scale.

## Why a Punchline Works (or Fails)

**Objective criteria (score 0-10 each)**:

- Surprise (unexpected)
- Recognition (truthful observation)
- Truth (specific, not generic)
- Safety (benign)
- Economy (concise)

Total **>= 35/50** marks a strong joke; below 30 is a rewrite candidate.

**Diagnostic checklist for weak punchlines**:

- [ ] Is the audience ahead of the character? (Kill or reverse.)
- [ ] Does it punch down on an unprotected target? (Add vulnerability.)
- [ ] Is the setup too long? (Cut 30%.)
- [ ] Does the reaction undercut the laugh? (Remove or heighten it.)

**Fix techniques**: add specificity ("not just a bad date -- a bad date
with my ex's new boyfriend who is my boss"), heighten stakes, add a tag.

**Audience psychology**: Modern audiences reward vulnerability and
self-awareness over meanness; topical material works best when tied to
character pain.

## Satire & Anti-Satire

### Satire

- **Description**: Attack an institution or behavior through exaggeration,
  irony, and a clear moral core.
- **Why It Matters**: Pointed targets keep satire honest; exaggeration
  without bite is noise, and a message without craft is a lecture.
- **Examples**:
  - ✅ **Good**: Cold War logic carried to nuclear absurdity with
    internally consistent procedure.
  - ❌ **Bad**: Preachy monologue where message overrides the laugh.
- **Film Reference**: Dr. Strangelove (1964).

### Anti-Satire

- **Description**: When cynicism is expected, deliver a sincere moment
  as the biggest punch.
- **Why It Matters**: Earnestness against jaded context reads as the
  freshest violation available.
- **Examples**:
  - ✅ **Good**: A coach's optimism disarms a room of cynics.
  - ❌ **Bad**: Saccharine sincerity without an ironic frame to subvert.
- **Film Reference**: Ted Lasso -- optimism weaponized.

## Slapstick & Physical Comedy

### Pain + Recovery

- **Description**: Cartoon physics for big laughs, grounded consequences
  for character; the audience must believe pain but not permanent
  damage (unless the sub-genre allows).
- **Why It Matters**: Recovery is the safety that makes the violation
  benign; lasting injury breaks the emotional contract.
- **Examples**:
  - ✅ **Good**: A pratfall with a visible setup -- the ignored
    wet-floor sign.
  - ❌ **Bad**: An injury that keeps the character out of the next beat.
- **Film Reference**: Hot Fuzz (2007) -- supermarket fight.

### Prop & Chain Reactions

- **Description**: Escalating misuse of specific objects; the camera
  frames timing and reaction as much as action.
- **Why It Matters**: Specificity names the gag; vague action lines
  flatten physical comedy.
- **Examples**:
  - ✅ **Good**: Three named props in one location escalate from mishap
    to full destruction.
  - ❌ **Bad**: "They do something funny with the cake."
- **Film Reference**: The Naked Gun -- choreographed chain reactions.

## Deadpan & Understatement

- **Description**: Maximum chaos described with minimum emotion; formal
  language held against absurd situations.
- **Why It Matters**: The contrast is the joke; deadpan lands hardest
  when surrounded by chaotic characters who refuse to match the tone.
- **Examples**:
  - ✅ **Good**: A flat "No, no, no..." while the room burns.
  - ❌ **Bad**: Deadpan in a room of deadpan -- no contrast engine.
- **Film Reference**: Fleabag -- title character's asides during implosion.

## Meta-Humor & Fourth-Wall Breaks

- **Description**: Acknowledge the form or genre, but only when it
  serves character or comments on audience expectations.
- **Why It Matters**: Meta that escapes plot problems drains momentum;
  meta that deepens character earns the break.
- **Examples**:
  - ✅ **Good**: A genre-parody episode that still advances emotional
    arcs for every player in the ensemble.
  - ❌ **Bad**: Winking asides used to bail out a stuck scene.
- **Film Reference**: Community -- the paintball episodes.

## Callbacks & Collaborative Humor

- **Description**: Ensemble "yes-and" on the page -- one character sets
  up, another punches, a third tops; running gags vary each payoff and
  are tracked in a dedicated gag bible.
- **Why It Matters**: Collaborative rhythm turns a room into a comedy
  engine; repeated gags survive only when surprise and familiarity mix.
- **Examples**:
  - ✅ **Good**: A tag-team joke where each voice adds a distinct angle.
  - ❌ **Bad**: One character hogging setup and punch alike.
- **Film Reference**: It's Always Sunny in Philadelphia -- the recurring
  "the gang solves [problem]" format.

## Advanced & Hybrid Techniques

- **Cringe comedy**: second-hand embarrassment as primary engine
  (*The Office*, *Curb Your Enthusiasm*).
- **Dark comedy**: violation without full safety (*Fargo*, *Barry*).
- **Absurdism**: logic breaks entirely (*Monty Python*, *Rick and Morty*).
- **Parody**: specific target with visible affection for the source.
- **Genre blending**: horror-comedy beats must satisfy both genres
  (*What We Do in the Shadows*).

The best comedies are both character- and situation-driven: the
situation exposes the flaw; the character creates the next situation.

## Validation & Revision Checklists

### Writer's Checklist (Human Pass)

- [ ] Every major sequence has a clear escalation curve.
- [ ] At least 70% of jokes tie to character flaw or premise.
- [ ] No unexplained punchlines -- every payoff is planted.
- [ ] Mix of verbal, visual, physical, and situational comedy.
- [ ] Read aloud: minimum 4 big laughs per 10 pages.
- [ ] Satire (if used) has an identifiable target and moral core.
- [ ] Deadpan/chaos contrast present in ensemble scenes.
- [ ] Final act inverts the original comedic premise.

### AI Output Checklist (Programmatic Pass)

- [ ] Punchline count >= 12 per 10 pages.
- [ ] Average joke score >= 36/50 across the five-criteria rubric.
- [ ] Each sequence measurably funnier than the previous.
- [ ] Minimum 3 callbacks resolved per act.
- [ ] No standalone jokes -- all advance A/B/C stories.
- [ ] Benign violation confirmed in every major comedic beat.
- [ ] Screenplay format: parentheticals for timing, action lines for
      visual gags, tone matched to the chosen style guide.

Run both checklists after every pass. Comedy is iterative -- plan for
3-5 revision cycles focused on heightening laughs while protecting
story and heart.
