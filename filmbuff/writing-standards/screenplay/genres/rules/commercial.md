# Commercial Genre

**Category**: Screenplay Genre  
**Type**: Primary  
**Complexity**: High

## Overview

Commercial screenwriting is the art of persuasive storytelling within strict time constraints — typically 15, 30, or 60 seconds. The genre fuses cinematic craft with marketing science: every image, word, and sound must earn its place by capturing attention, communicating a single clear benefit, and compelling the viewer to act.

## Core Concept

A commercial is a complete dramatic arc compressed to the length of a pop song. Success depends on an irresistible hook in the first two to three seconds, a problem-solution-resolution structure that never wastes a frame, and a crystal-clear call-to-action that tells the viewer exactly what to do next. Unlike feature films, the commercial writer serves two masters simultaneously — the audience's emotional needs and the client's business objectives — and must satisfy both without either noticing the seam.

## Core Rules

### Rule 1: One Message Per Spot

**Description**: Every commercial must carry exactly one core message. The moment a second idea is introduced, both are weakened.

**Why It Matters**: Viewers retain roughly one idea from a 30-second spot. Trying to communicate two messages halves the impact of each.

**Examples**:
- ✅ **Good**: "The new Apex sedan. The safest car we've ever built." — all visuals and copy support safety only
- ❌ **Bad**: "Safe, efficient, stylish, and affordable!" — four messages, zero memory

**Film Reference**: Apple "1984" (1984) — one message: IBM conformity ends today. Nothing else.

---

### Rule 2: Hook in the First Three Seconds

**Description**: The hook — visual, audio, or narrative — must land before the viewer's attention drifts or the skip button is pressed.

**Why It Matters**: Streaming pre-roll ads can be skipped after five seconds; social video loses 30–40% of viewers in the first three. If the hook isn't instant, the spot doesn't exist.

**Examples**:
- ✅ **Good**: Open on complete silence in a loud environment; whispered VO: "Noise-canceling headphones that actually work."
- ❌ **Bad**: Open on establishing shot of headquarters, then logo reveal, then product intro

**Film Reference**: Old Spice "The Man Your Man Could Smell Like" (2010) — "Hello, ladies" in frame one.

---

### Rule 3: Problem → Solution → CTA (3-Act Compression)

**Description**: Adapt the three-act structure to the spot's duration: Act I establishes a relatable problem (first 20–30%), Act II presents the product as the solution (middle 40–50%), Act III delivers a clear call-to-action (final 20–30%).

**Why It Matters**: Human brains are wired for narrative. Even a 15-second spot with a problem and a resolution feels satisfying; a 15-second spot that's just product features feels like noise.

**Examples**:
- ✅ **Good**: Woman drowning in paperwork (problem) → opens MediFlow on laptop (solution) → "Try free at MediFlow.com" (CTA)
- ❌ **Bad**: Voiceover listing seven software features over product screenshots

**Film Reference**: Wendy's "Where's the Beef?" (1984) — tiny patty (problem), Wendy's burger (solution), brand recall (CTA).

---

### Rule 4: Emotional Connection Before Logic

**Description**: Lead with one primary emotion — humor, nostalgia, fear, joy, or aspiration — and let the rational product benefits follow as justification.

**Why It Matters**: People buy emotionally and justify logically. A commercial that leads with specs and ends with a tagline is the reverse of how human decision-making actually works.

**Examples**:
- ✅ **Good**: Elderly grandfather video-calls grandchildren (joy/connection) → "Stay connected. FamilyConnect."
- ❌ **Bad**: "Our plan offers 5G speeds, 50GB data, and 99.9% uptime." (logic-first, no emotional hook)

**Film Reference**: Budweiser "Puppy Love" (2014, Super Bowl) — pure emotional story; brand appears only at the end.

---

### Rule 5: Brand Must Be Unmistakable

**Description**: Viewers must be able to recall who the commercial was for. Brand identity — name, logo, sonic mark, or distinctive visual — must be woven throughout, not just slapped on at the end.

**Why It Matters**: A beautifully made commercial that people remember as "that funny ad" rather than "that funny Geico ad" wastes the entire media buy.

**Examples**:
- ✅ **Good**: Brand colours and logo integrated naturally at hook, midpoint, and CTA
- ❌ **Bad**: Emotionally compelling story followed by a two-second logo card no one processes

**Film Reference**: Geico — the Gecko mascot is the first and last thing you see, reinforcing brand on every impression.

---

### Rule 6: Write for the Clock, Not the Page

**Description**: Commercial copy must be timed precisely. At a standard pace, 30 seconds = 60–75 words of voiceover. Every word of VO must be read aloud with a stopwatch.

**Why It Matters**: Overwritten scripts create rushed delivery that destroys emotional tone; underwritten scripts leave dead air that signals unprofessionalism.

**Examples**:
- ✅ **Good**: 30-second spot VO at 68 words, read aloud in 28 seconds — two seconds of breathing room
- ❌ **Bad**: 110-word VO for a 30-second spot, delivered at auctioneer speed

**Film Reference**: Any Apple product launch spot — VO is sparse, silence carries as much weight as words.

## Guidelines

### Structure

- **Act I (Hook/Problem)**: First 20–30% — visual or narrative hook + relatable problem or desire
- **Act II (Solution/Demo)**: Middle 40–50% — product introduced as solution; key benefit shown, not told
- **Act III (CTA)**: Final 20–30% — single clear call-to-action, brand reinforcement, urgency if appropriate

### Pacing

- **:15 spot**: One idea, three beats — hook, benefit, CTA. No room for story.
- **:30 spot**: Simple narrative arc; problem-solution-CTA with one character turn
- **:60 spot**: Full emotional journey; character, conflict, transformation, CTA
- Match edit pace to emotional tone — fast cuts for energy, slow shots for sentiment
- Music tempo drives perceived pacing; choose track before picture lock

### Character Archetypes

- **Protagonist**: The target customer — relatable, demographically matched, experiencing the problem
- **Antagonist**: The problem itself (clutter, pain, inefficiency, anxiety) or a rival product
- **Supporting**: Expert, celebrity endorser, or satisfied customer who validates the solution

### Tone and Atmosphere

- Select ONE dominant emotion per spot: humor, warmth, urgency, aspiration, nostalgia, or reassurance
- Brand voice (established in brand guidelines) overrides individual creative tone
- Avoid tonal ambiguity — a spot that's 60% funny and 40% earnest reads as confused
- Match production value to brand positioning: luxury brands demand restraint; youth brands demand energy

### Visual Style

- Two-column script format (VISUAL | AUDIO) is the industry standard for TV and streaming
- Every visual element must earn its place by supporting the single core message
- Product must appear in its best light — literal and figurative
- Text overlays (SUPER) for URL, phone number, offer details — large, high-contrast, on screen ≥ 3 seconds
- Brand colours and logo follow brand guidelines exactly

### Dialogue

- Prefer voiceover over on-screen dialogue when pacing or montage structure requires it
- Write VO for the ear: short sentences, contractions, no jargon
- On-screen dialogue should feel overheard, not performed — testimonials especially
- Tagline is the last words the viewer hears; make it the most memorable line in the script

## Common Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Feature Dumping

**Problem**: Listing every product attribute instead of dramatising one benefit.

**Solution**: Identify the single feature that matters most to the target audience and build the entire spot around proving that one claim visually.

**Example**: Dyson vacuum ads never list suction specs; they show a carpet before and after.

---

### Pitfall 2: Weak or Missing CTA

**Problem**: The spot is memorable but viewers don't know what to do next.

**Solution**: The CTA must use an action verb, a specific destination (URL, phone, app), and urgency language if available. State it in VO and display it on screen simultaneously.

**Example**: "Visit Nike.com/air-max — this week only" beats "Learn more at our website."

---

### Pitfall 3: Slow Open

**Problem**: Establishing shot, title card, or brand logo appears before any hook.

**Solution**: Cut directly to the dramatic moment. Brand identity can come at second five or at the end; the hook must be frame one.

**Example**: Dollar Shave Club "Our Blades Are F***ing Great" (2012) — CEO talking directly to camera from word one.

---

### Pitfall 4: Unbranded Storytelling

**Problem**: The emotional story is so self-contained that the product feels like an afterthought.

**Solution**: Integrate the brand organically into the emotional resolution — the product is the reason the character succeeds.

**Example**: The Hallmark-style emotional spot that nobody can attribute to any specific brand the next day.

## Film Examples

### Example 1: Apple "1984" (1984)

**Director**: Ridley Scott

**Why It Works**: Cinematic production, a single political metaphor, zero product demo. Communicated an entire brand identity in 60 seconds.

**Key Scenes**:
- **The Runner**: Visual hook — woman in colour against grey conformity
- **The Hammer**: Single explosive CTA moment — "1984 won't be like '1984'"

**Techniques Used**:
- Aspirational emotional trigger (liberation)
- Problem (IBM conformity) dramatised without naming the competitor
- Brand appears only as a final card — after the story has done its work

---

### Example 2: Old Spice "The Man Your Man Could Smell Like" (2010)

**Director**: Tom Kuntz

**Why It Works**: Absurdist humour, single unbroken take, direct address to a dual audience (men and women). Viral before viral was a strategy.

**Key Scenes**:
- **"Hello, Ladies"**: Instant hook via unexpected direct address
- **Horse Reveal**: Visual escalation that rewards continued watching

**Techniques Used**:
- Humor as the primary emotional trigger
- No product demo — the product IS the absurdity
- One continuous shot eliminates editing as a crutch

---

### Example 3: Dollar Shave Club "Our Blades Are F***ing Great" (2012)

**Director**: Georgie Jeffery

**Why It Works**: Founder on camera, no-budget aesthetic that became the brand identity, CTA baked directly into the title.

**Key Scenes**:
- **Opening monologue**: Problem stated and solved in 20 seconds
- **Warehouse tour**: Product demo as comedy bit

**Techniques Used**:
- Direct address — founder as protagonist
- Conversational VO writing (feels improvised, was scripted)
- URL as the CTA repeated throughout

---

### Example 4: Budweiser "Puppy Love" (2014)

**Director**: Jake Scott

**Why It Works**: Emotional storytelling with zero product messaging until the final frame. Proves that brand recall can be earned through pure emotion.

**Key Scenes**:
- **Puppy-Clydesdale friendship**: Emotional hook in the first five seconds
- **Reunion**: Payoff that earns brand reveal

**Techniques Used**:
- Joy and warmth as primary emotional triggers
- Product mention deferred until after emotional peak
- Music ("Let Her Go") carries the entire emotional arc

## Integration with Other Features

### Compatible Themes

- **Aspiration**: Products that improve lifestyle or status
- **Problem/Relief**: Pain points resolved — cleaning, health, finance, productivity
- **Community**: Brand as shared identity ("We are the people who use X")

### Compatible Styles

- **Testimonial**: Real customer voice adds credibility to product claims
- **Demonstration**: Before/after, stress tests, side-by-side comparisons
- **Narrative**: Character-driven mini-story; emotional journey ending at product

### Hybrid Combinations

- **Commercial + Documentary**: Brand documentary (mini-doc format; Nike "Breaking2")
- **Commercial + Comedy**: Humor-driven spots; absurdist brand mascots (Geico, Progressive Flo)
- **Commercial + Drama**: Emotional narrative spots; holiday films (John Lewis, Budweiser)
- **Commercial + Social**: :15 cut-downs and vertical reformats for TikTok/Reels distribution

## Best Practices

1. **One message**: Define it before writing word one; every element must serve it
2. **Hook first**: Dramatic moment in the first 3 seconds, no exceptions
3. **Time your VO**: Read aloud with a stopwatch; overwriting kills pacing
4. **Earn the CTA**: The viewer must feel the product solves a real problem before you ask for action
5. **Integrate the brand**: Logo and name woven through the spot, not only at the end
6. **Match emotion to audience**: Know which of the six core emotions (humor, nostalgia, fear, joy, aspiration, relief) resonates with your target demographic
7. **Write for platform**: :15 for pre-roll; :30 for broadcast; :60 for storytelling; vertical 9:16 for social
8. **Demonstrate, don't claim**: Show the product working; avoid adjectives that audiences discount ("amazing," "revolutionary")
9. **Legal first**: Run every claim past legal before production; testimonials require disclosure
10. **Test hooks**: A/B test opening three seconds before committing to full production
