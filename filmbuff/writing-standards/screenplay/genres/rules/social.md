# Social Genre

**Category**: Screenplay Genre  
**Type**: Primary  
**Complexity**: Medium

## Overview

Social/short-form video is storytelling engineered for algorithm-driven platforms — TikTok, Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts, and Facebook Stories. The genre is defined by extreme brevity (15 seconds to 3 minutes), vertical framing (9:16), an irresistible hook in the first three seconds, and a call-to-action that drives the platform engagement metrics that determine distribution reach.

## Core Concept

Social video is the most compressed storytelling format in cinema history. Every creative choice is simultaneously a creative and algorithmic decision: the hook determines whether the platform shows the video to more people; the completion rate determines whether it goes viral. Great social content looks effortless and improvised but is structurally precise — a single concept, executed with maximum personality, delivered at a pace that never lets the viewer's thumb win. The writer must simultaneously be a screenwriter, a UX designer, and a platform strategist.

## Core Rules

### Rule 1: The Three-Second Rule

**Description**: The hook — the first visual frame or the first spoken word — must create an immediate reason to keep watching. If the first three seconds don't earn the next three, the video is dead.

**Why It Matters**: TikTok and Instagram Reels surface completion rate as the primary ranking signal. A video that loses 40% of viewers in the first three seconds will never be recommended. The hook IS the pitch.

**Examples**:
- ✅ **Good**: Open mid-action — "I just quit my job and this is what happened" over dramatic face close-up
- ❌ **Bad**: Fade in, channel intro jingle, "Hey guys, welcome back, today we're going to talk about..."

**Film Reference**: MrBeast — every video opens on the most extreme moment, then walks back to explain it.

---

### Rule 2: One Concept Per Video

**Description**: Social video carries exactly one idea, one story beat, or one joke. The moment a second concept is introduced, viewers disengage.

**Why It Matters**: Short-form audiences scroll with intention. A clear, singular concept telegraphs its value immediately; a multi-part idea reads as unfocused and loses the viewer before the payoff.

**Examples**:
- ✅ **Good**: "The correct way to fold a fitted sheet" — one skill, one payoff, one CTA
- ❌ **Bad**: "Fitted sheet folding, plus three other laundry hacks, plus product review, plus Q&A"

**Film Reference**: Khaby Lame — one absurd over-complication, one simple correction, zero words needed.

---

### Rule 3: Vertical First, Horizontal Never

**Description**: All visual composition must be designed natively for 9:16 vertical format. Center key elements, use the full vertical space, keep text in safe zones away from UI overlays.

**Why It Matters**: Horizontal video on a vertical platform forces the viewer to rotate their device or watch a letterboxed thumbnail. Both experiences signal low production quality and reduce watch time.

**Examples**:
- ✅ **Good**: Face close-up centered in frame; text in top and bottom thirds clear of platform UI
- ❌ **Bad**: Landscape video cropped to vertical, cutting off half the frame

**Film Reference**: Zach King — every video is composed for vertical first; the magic trick only works in the native format.

---

### Rule 4: Tease the Payoff, Then Deliver It

**Description**: State or imply the payoff at the hook, then make the viewer watch the entire video to receive it. This is the "curiosity loop" that drives completion rate.

**Why It Matters**: Completion rate is the most important algorithmic signal on every short-form platform. A video that earns its runtime by promising and then delivering a satisfying payoff will be re-watched and shared.

**Examples**:
- ✅ **Good**: "Wait for it..." or "The ending will surprise you" — explicit payoff promise that creates obligation to watch
- ❌ **Bad**: Slow buildup with no payoff tease; viewers leave before they know what the video is about

**Film Reference**: Any Zach King "magic vine" — the hook shows something physically impossible; the payoff explains the trick.

---

### Rule 5: Pattern Interrupts Every 5–10 Seconds

**Description**: Change something on screen every 5–10 seconds — cut to a new angle, add a text overlay, change location, introduce a sound effect. Prevent the viewer's brain from going on autopilot.

**Why It Matters**: The scroll is always one thumb-swipe away. Pattern interrupts reset the viewer's attention each time it starts to drift, effectively extending perceived watch time without adding duration.

**Examples**:
- ✅ **Good**: Cut → text overlay → cut → B-roll → cut → reaction shot → text → CTA
- ❌ **Bad**: Single locked-off camera angle for 60 seconds with only voiceover

**Film Reference**: Emma Chamberlain popularised the jump-cut style that became the default social edit grammar.

---

### Rule 6: End with a Clear CTA

**Description**: Every social video must end with an explicit call-to-action — like, comment, follow, share, visit a link. The CTA should be verbally stated and displayed as text simultaneously.

**Why It Matters**: Platform algorithms weight engagement signals (comments, shares, saves) as heavily as completion rate. A video that doesn't ask for engagement is leaving algorithmic reach on the table.

**Examples**:
- ✅ **Good**: "Drop your answer in the comments" / "Follow for part 2" / "Save this for later"
- ❌ **Bad**: Video ends abruptly with no instruction; viewer scrolls on without engaging

**Film Reference**: Every successful YouTube channel ends with a verbal + visual CTA — the subscribe button, the playlist card, the comment prompt.

## Guidelines

### Structure

- **Hook (0–3s)**: Dramatic opening statement, visual, or action that creates immediate curiosity
- **Setup (3–10s)**: Establish the premise — what problem is being solved or what story is being told
- **Development (10s – payoff -5s)**: Escalate, demonstrate, or build to the promised payoff; pattern interrupts every 5–10s
- **Payoff**: Deliver the punchline, reveal, transformation, or resolution — the emotional peak
- **CTA (final 3s)**: Verbal and visual call-to-action (follow, comment, share, save, link in bio)

### Pacing

- Ultra-short (:15–:30): Hook + single beat + CTA. No room for setup; open on the action
- Short (1–3 min): Hook + promise + development + payoff + CTA; one cut or transition every 3–5 seconds
- Never use a slow intro — the platform sees drop-off in the first three seconds as a death signal
- Match edit pace to audio: trending sounds dictate rhythm; build to musical peak for the payoff
- Use captions for all speech — 80% of social video is watched with sound off

### Character Archetypes

- **Creator/Host**: The face of the content; must establish personality in the first three seconds; authenticity trumps polish
- **Subject**: The person, object, or idea being demonstrated or reacted to
- **Audience surrogate**: The comment section — successful social video anticipates and answers the viewer's unspoken question

### Tone and Atmosphere

- Authenticity over production value — a well-lit smartphone video outperforms an over-produced corporate spot
- Match tone to platform: TikTok rewards absurdist humour and raw authenticity; Instagram Reels rewards aspirational aesthetics; YouTube Shorts rewards value-driven education
- Energy must be high from frame one — there is no warmup period
- First-person direct address is the default register; "I" and "you" are more effective than third person

### Visual Style

- **Vertical format (9:16)**: Non-negotiable primary composition; design for the phone held upright
- **Face close-ups**: Human faces drive retention; fill the vertical frame with the creator's face when speaking
- **Text overlays**: Captions and key-word emphasis in large, readable fonts; animated text over static text
- **Quick cuts**: Standard edit rhythm is 2–4 seconds per shot in fast-paced content
- **B-roll**: Relevant cutaways reinforce points and break up talking-head monotony

### Dialogue

- Conversational, informal register — contractions, slang, first-person address
- Speak to one person, not an audience: "you" not "everyone watching"
- Write for ears AND eyes simultaneously — the spoken word should complement, not duplicate, the on-screen text
- Trending audio: using a viral sound on TikTok/Reels gets the video served to fans of that sound first

## Common Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: The Slow Intro

**Problem**: Creator intro, channel jingle, "Hey everyone," or any non-hook content in the first three seconds.

**Solution**: Start in media res — mid-action, mid-sentence, or with the most dramatic moment of the video. Introduce yourself after you've earned the viewer's attention.

**Example**: Cut directly to the result of the recipe, then reveal how you made it — not the other way around.

---

### Pitfall 2: Horizontal-Native Composition

**Problem**: Video filmed or edited in landscape (16:9) then posted vertically, leaving dead space or cutting off important content.

**Solution**: Shoot natively vertical. If repurposing horizontal content, use a vertical frame with blurred or colourful background fill behind the letterboxed image.

**Example**: Any brand that reposts its TV commercial to TikTok without reformatting it for vertical.

---

### Pitfall 3: No Payoff

**Problem**: The video builds curiosity but delivers an underwhelming or non-existent payoff.

**Solution**: Identify the payoff first and build the entire video backward from it. If the payoff doesn't justify the viewer's time investment, the video concept is wrong.

**Example**: "You won't believe what I found" videos that reveal something completely unremarkable — high drop-off, negative comment sentiment, algorithmic suppression.

---

### Pitfall 4: Missing Captions

**Problem**: All dialogue is spoken audio only; no captions or text overlays.

**Solution**: Add captions to all speech. Auto-caption tools (CapCut, Descript, TikTok's native tool) make this trivial. Without captions, the video is invisible to deaf viewers and to anyone watching with sound off.

**Example**: Industry data consistently shows 10–30% lift in completion rate when captions are added.

## Film Examples

### Example 1: MrBeast — "I Spent 50 Hours Buried Alive" (YouTube, 2021)

**Creator**: Jimmy Donaldson (MrBeast)

**Why It Works**: Extreme premise established in the title; the hook is the most dramatic moment; pacing is relentless with pattern interrupts every few seconds.

**Key Scenes**:
- **Opening**: Shows the burial first — not the preparation
- **Midpoint escalation**: Physical discomfort dramatised to raise stakes
- **Resolution**: Survival payoff earns the emotional release

**Techniques Used**:
- Extreme premise as irresistible hook
- Multi-camera coverage for constant pattern interrupts
- Comment-bait CTA ("Could you survive this?")

---

### Example 2: Khaby Lame — "Life Hack" Series (TikTok, 2021–present)

**Creator**: Khaby Lame

**Why It Works**: Zero dialogue, universal format — over-complicated "life hack" followed by Khaby's silent, obvious correction. Transcends language barriers; globally viral.

**Key Scenes**:
- **Complicated hack**: Sets up the absurdity in 5–10 seconds
- **Khaby's correction**: The payoff is the face — disappointment and incredulity

**Techniques Used**:
- One-concept format executed without variation
- Reaction shot as the entire payoff
- No verbal CTA needed — the share-worthy format IS the CTA

---

### Example 3: Zach King — Magic Vine Series (TikTok/Instagram)

**Creator**: Zach King

**Why It Works**: Each video is a single impossible visual trick. The hook is the trick; the payoff is the reveal. Designed for rewatching — the primary signal for algorithmic amplification.

**Key Scenes**:
- **The Impossible Moment**: Hook that defies physics — earns immediate "wait, what?" response
- **The Reveal**: Clever camera or editing trick explained or left mysterious

**Techniques Used**:
- Rewatch bait — the best videos reward a second viewing
- Native vertical composition — tricks designed around the phone frame
- No dialogue; pure visual storytelling

---

### Example 4: Emma Chamberlain — Vlog Format (YouTube)

**Creator**: Emma Chamberlain

**Why It Works**: Defined the jump-cut vlog aesthetic that became social video's dominant grammar. Authenticity over polish; personality over production value.

**Key Scenes**:
- **Direct address**: Emma speaking directly to camera from frame one
- **Jump cuts**: Ellipsis editing that cuts all dead air — every cut tightens the energy

**Techniques Used**:
- Jump-cut editing as creative choice, not mistake
- Self-deprecating humour builds parasocial intimacy
- No formal CTA — subscriber loyalty replaces algorithmic CTA

## Integration with Other Features

### Compatible Themes

- **Transformation**: Before/after; skill acquired; problem solved — the most shareable social arc
- **Authenticity**: Behind-the-scenes; "day in the life"; confession format
- **Community**: Comment engagement bait; response videos; challenges

### Compatible Styles

- **Educational**: Tutorial, how-to, "did you know" — value-driven content that earns saves
- **Entertaining**: Comedy sketches, reaction content, absurdist humour
- **Inspirational**: Transformation stories, motivation, achievement — earns shares

### Hybrid Combinations

- **Social + Commercial**: :15 UGC-style ad; brand integration that looks organic; influencer partnership format
- **Social + Documentary**: Short-form mini-doc; "the story of..." format; investigative reel
- **Social + Comedy**: Sketch format; character series; absurdist single-take concept

## Best Practices

1. **Hook first**: Write the first three seconds last — after you know the whole video, find its most dramatic moment and put it first
2. **One concept**: If you can't summarise the video in six words, cut until you can
3. **Vertical native**: Compose for 9:16 at every stage from script to export
4. **Caption everything**: Add captions before publishing, without exception
5. **Pattern interrupt**: Something must change on screen every 5–10 seconds
6. **Tease the payoff**: State or imply the payoff at the hook; don't bury the lead
7. **End with CTA**: Always ask for one specific engagement action — comment, save, follow, or share
8. **Trending audio**: Use platform-native trending audio to get surfaced to the sound's audience
9. **Study your analytics**: Drop-off rate and completion rate reveal exactly where the video breaks
10. **Post consistently**: Algorithm favour goes to accounts that post regularly; consistency beats perfection
